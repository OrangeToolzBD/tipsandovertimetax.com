How No Tax on Tips Works

No tax on tips lets workers in tipped occupations deduct their voluntary, reported tips from federal taxable income - up to $25,000 a year for tax years 2025-2028. You need a valid Social Security number, married couples must file jointly, and the deduction phases out above $150,000 (single) or $300,000 (joint) of income.

Which jobs qualify

The deduction is limited to occupations that customarily and regularly receive tips. The IRS publishes the qualifying list, which covers front-of-house restaurant staff, bartenders, baristas, hairstylists, barbers, nail and beauty technicians, delivery drivers, valets, hotel housekeepers, and similar tipped roles.

If your job isn't on that list, tips you happen to receive generally don't qualify - the rule targets industries where tipping is a normal part of pay. A manager who occasionally gets a tip, for instance, is unlikely to qualify unless their role is a listed tipped occupation.

Two eligibility rules apply on top: you must have a valid Social Security number, and if you're married you must file jointly to claim the deduction. Before you rely on it, confirm your occupation appears on the current IRS list in the official guidance, since the list can be updated.

Which tips count (voluntary, reported)

Only voluntary tips count - money a customer freely chooses to leave. A mandatory service charge or auto-gratuity that the business adds to the bill is treated as regular wages, not a tip, and doesn't qualify for the deduction.

Tips must also be reported. For employees that means the tips shown on your W-2; for self-employed workers it means the tips you report as business income. Cash tips you never reported can't be deducted, and trying to add unreported income at tax time creates its own problems.

Qualifying tips include cash, card and pooled tips shared among staff, as long as they're voluntary and reported. Keep a daily record so your reported figure is accurate and matches what your employer sends to the IRS. The calculator uses your reported W-2 and self-employed tip figures to estimate the deduction.

Cap and income limit

The deduction is capped at $25,000 of qualifying tips per year. If you report more than that, only $25,000 is deductible. On top of the cap, there's an income phase-out: above a modified adjusted gross income of $150,000 (single) or $300,000 (joint), the deduction is gradually reduced.

Most tipped workers earn well below those thresholds, so they receive the full deduction up to the cap. The precise per-dollar phase-out rate comes from the IRS Schedule 1-A instructions, which we're confirming against that source - so near the thresholds, treat the number as an estimate.

The deduction is available whether you itemize or take the standard deduction, and it reduces federal income tax only - Social Security and Medicare are unaffected. Use the calculator to see how the cap and phase-out apply to your own reported tips and income.

Examples by job

Server. Reports $12,000 in W-2 tips, earns $45,000 total. Full $12,000 is deductible; in the 12% bracket that's about $1,440 saved.

Bartender. Reports $26,000 in tips. The deduction is capped at $25,000; the extra $1,000 stays taxable. In the 22% bracket the capped deduction saves about $5,500.

Hairstylist (self-employed). Reports $9,000 in tips from a chair rental business with $4,000 net profit. Self-employed tips can't exceed the business's net profit, so only $4,000 qualifies.

Delivery driver. Reports $7,000 in app tips, income well under the threshold - the full $7,000 is deductible. Run each in the calculator to see the saving at your bracket.

Self-employed and gig workers

Self-employed and gig workers - independent hairstylists, rideshare and delivery drivers, and others reported on a 1099 - can qualify, but with an extra limit: your deductible tips cannot exceed the net profit of the business the tips came from. If the work netted $4,000, you can't deduct $9,000 of tips against it.

You still need to be in a tipped occupation, have a valid Social Security number, and (if married) file jointly. Report your tips as part of your business income, and keep clean books so the tip figure and your net profit reconcile.

The deduction reduces income tax, but self-employment tax (Social Security and Medicare on net earnings) still applies. The calculator has a dedicated field for self-employed tips and caps them at the net profit you enter, then applies the overall $25,000 limit. Confirm details on IRS.gov.

Frequently asked questions

How does the No Tax on Tips work?

Qualifying voluntary, reported tips become deductible from federal taxable income, up to $25,000 a year for 2025-2028, claimed on Schedule 1-A. You still report your tips; the deduction lowers the income tax on them. It requires a valid SSN, joint filing for married couples, and phases out at higher incomes.

How is the No Tax on Tips going to work exactly now?

It's an above-the-line deduction you take at tax time, available whether or not you itemize. Employees use their W-2 tips; self-employed workers use reported tips limited to net profit. The $25,000 cap and the income phase-out apply, and only listed tipped occupations qualify.

What is the $600 rule?

The $600 rule usually refers to the 1099-K/1099 reporting threshold for payment platforms and contractors - separate from this deduction. It affects what income gets reported to the IRS, not whether your tips qualify. Reported tips are what count here, so accurate reporting still matters.

What's the downside to No Tax on Tips?

It only reduces federal income tax (not FICA or most state tax), it's capped at $25,000, it phases out above $150,000/$300,000, and it covers only voluntary tips in listed occupations. It's also temporary - 2025 through 2028 - unless extended, and unreported tips can't be claimed.

How much will I get back no tax on tips?

Your saving is your deductible tips times your tax bracket. For example $12,000 of tips in the 12% bracket saves about $1,440; the $25,000 cap in the 22% bracket saves about $5,500. Use the calculator with your reported tips and income for a personal estimate.

Who will benefit from no tax on tips?

Workers in tipped occupations who report their tips, have a valid SSN, and (if married) file jointly - mostly those earning below the phase-out thresholds. Servers, bartenders, stylists, delivery drivers and similar roles benefit most; high earners may be phased out.

How does the no tax on tips and overtime work?

They're two separate deductions from the same 2025 law: one for voluntary reported tips (up to $25,000) and one for the FLSA overtime premium (up to $12,500/$25,000). Both reduce federal income tax for 2025-2028, both phase out at higher incomes, and you can claim both if you earn both kinds of pay.

How is the no tax on tips going to work exactly now?

You report tips as normal during the year, then claim the deduction on Schedule 1-A when you file. Withholding on your paychecks may not change, so the benefit typically appears as a smaller balance due or a bigger refund. The cap, phase-out and occupation rules all apply.
