How No Tax on Overtime Works

No tax on overtime is a federal income-tax deduction on the premium half of your overtime pay — the extra amount above your regular rate — for tax years 2025–2028. You can deduct up to $12,500 (single) or $25,000 (joint), it phases out at higher incomes, and you claim it on Schedule 1-A whether or not you itemize.

Qualified overtime: FLSA overtime premium only

The deduction covers only the premium portion of overtime required under the Fair Labor Standards Act (FLSA) — the "half" in time-and-a-half, not your entire overtime paycheck. If your regular rate is $30 an hour and overtime pays $45, the qualifying premium is the extra $15 per hour.

That distinction matters. Someone who works a lot of overtime may see a large gross overtime figure, but only the premium above their normal rate is deductible. Overtime that isn't FLSA-required — for example, contractual overtime paid at your regular rate, or "overtime" that's really a shift differential — generally doesn't count as a premium.

Your employer identifies and reports qualifying overtime, and that figure flows to your tax return. When you use the calculator, entering your regular rate and overtime hours produces the premium automatically (rate × 0.5 × hours). Always match your entry to what appears on your pay records. See the IRS overview for the definition.

Income limit and phase-out

There are two limits. First, an annual cap: the deduction can't exceed $12,500 for single filers or $25,000 for married couples filing jointly. Second, an income phase-out: once your modified adjusted gross income (MAGI) rises above $150,000 (single) or $300,000 (joint), the deduction starts to shrink and eventually disappears.

MAGI for most people is close to the adjusted-gross-income line on Form 1040. Below the threshold you get the full deduction up to the cap; above it, the deduction reduces as income climbs. The exact per-dollar reduction rate is set in the IRS Schedule 1-A instructions, which we're confirming — so treat results near the thresholds as an estimate.

You also need a valid Social Security number, and married taxpayers must file jointly; married filing separately gets nothing. The calculator applies the cap, the phase-out and these eligibility gates so you can see the effect of your own income.

Three examples (hourly nurse, warehouse worker, married couple)

Hourly nurse. A nurse earning $40 an hour works 200 overtime hours. Premium = $40 × 0.5 × 200 = $4,000. With income under $150,000, the full $4,000 is deductible; in the 24% bracket that saves about $960.

Warehouse worker. At $25 an hour and 300 overtime hours, the premium is $3,750. Under the cap and threshold, the whole amount is deductible — about $825 saved in the 22% bracket.

Married couple. Two earners filing jointly with a combined $28,000 overtime premium are capped at $25,000. If their MAGI is under $300,000 there's no phase-out, so they deduct $25,000; in the 22% bracket that's roughly $5,500 in federal income tax. Tax years it applies to (2025–2028)

The overtime deduction is temporary: it applies to tax years 2025 through 2028. Overtime worked in 2025 is claimed on the return you file in early 2026, and so on through the 2028 tax year filed in 2029, unless Congress extends or changes the rule.

Because it's a deduction claimed at filing time, you don't need to do anything special during the year beyond keeping your pay records. Withholding on your paychecks may not change, so the benefit typically shows up as a smaller balance due or a larger refund when you file.

Each tax year is separate, with its own cap and its own income test. If your situation changes — more overtime, a raise that pushes you toward the phase-out, a change in filing status — re-run the calculator for that year. Always confirm the current-year rules on IRS.gov.

Common mistakes

The most common error is deducting the whole overtime paycheck instead of just the premium half — only the extra above your regular rate qualifies. Another is forgetting the deduction is federal income tax only: Social Security, Medicare and most state taxes still apply, so your overtime isn't truly "tax-free."

Married taxpayers sometimes file separately and lose the deduction entirely — you must file jointly. Others overlook the income phase-out and expect the full cap despite a high MAGI, or try to claim it without a valid Social Security number.

Finally, don't assume a fixed refund figure you saw online applies to you; the benefit depends on your premium, your bracket and your income. Use the calculator for your own estimate, and check with a tax professional before filing.

Frequently asked questions

How much money will I get back for no tax on overtime?

Your benefit equals the deductible overtime premium times your federal tax bracket, up to the annual cap. A $4,000 premium in the 22% bracket is about $880; hitting the $12,500 cap in the 24% bracket is about $3,000. It shows up as a lower tax bill or bigger refund, not extra pay each week.

Will my tax return be bigger with no tax on overtime?

It can be. The deduction lowers your taxable income, which can increase your refund or reduce what you owe — depending on your withholding and total income. The overtime is still income, and FICA still applies, so the effect is limited to federal income tax up to the cap.

Is no tax on overtime a good thing?

For hourly workers who log significant overtime and earn below the phase-out, it's a real tax cut on the premium half of that pay. Its limits — the caps, the phase-out, federal-income-tax-only, and a 2025–2028 window — mean the size of the benefit varies a lot from person to person.

How much of overtime pay will not be taxed?

Only the FLSA premium (the "half" of time-and-a-half) is deductible, up to $12,500 single or $25,000 joint per year, and only for federal income tax. Your base overtime pay, and all of it for Social Security and Medicare, is still taxed as normal.

What is the downside of no tax on overtime?

It doesn't touch payroll taxes or most state taxes, it's capped, it phases out at higher incomes, and it's temporary (2025–2028). It also requires a valid SSN and joint filing for married couples. For high earners the phase-out can reduce or eliminate it entirely.

Will I get a bigger tax refund if I work overtime?

The deduction can increase your refund because it reduces taxable income, but working overtime still raises your total income and payroll taxes. Whether your refund grows depends on how much of your overtime is deductible premium, your bracket, and how much tax was withheld during the year.

How will the no tax on overtime work in 2026?

Identically to 2025: a deduction on your overtime premium up to $12,500/$25,000, phasing out above $150,000/$300,000 of income, claimed on Schedule 1-A. You'll claim 2026 overtime when you file in early 2027. Estimate it now with your expected 2026 rate and hours.

Who qualifies for the no tax on overtime?

Workers who receive FLSA-required overtime premiums, have a valid Social Security number, and — if married — file jointly. The full benefit goes to those with income below $150,000 (single) or $300,000 (joint); above that it phases out. It's available whether you itemize or take the standard deduction.
